Most people arrive at this question from one of two directions. Either a student has been assessed, dictation has been recommended as a reasonable adjustment, and someone now has to work out what to buy — or a student has been quietly struggling to get words onto the page for years, and the family has finally gone looking for something that helps.

Either way the question is the same: Dragon Professional 16 sits at A$994 at full retail and A$545 on the educational licence. Is that a sensible thing to spend on a student, or is it more software than the situation calls for?

The honest answer depends almost entirely on one distinction, and it is worth getting straight before any money changes hands.

## What the Educational Licence Actually Gets You

The educational and student licence is the same Dragon Professional 16 software as the full retail product. It is not a cut-down edition. What differs is the price and the eligibility — it is intended for students and academic use, which is why it sits at A$545 rather than A$994.

Two things about it matter more than the discount.

**It is a perpetual licence.** You buy it once and you own that version outright. There is no annual renewal, and if the student uses it through the back half of secondary school and into a degree, the cost per year of actual use falls a long way. For a student with a long, documented need, that is the strongest argument for buying Dragon rather than renting something.

**It does considerably more than dictate.** This is the part that gets lost in price comparisons. Dragon is a full voice-control environment: navigating Windows, controlling the mouse, correcting by voice, building custom commands and macros, and working entirely offline without an internet connection. For a student who cannot comfortably use a keyboard and mouse — a motor impairment, a chronic pain condition, an injury, significant fatigue — that depth is the whole point, and nothing at the budget end of the market replaces it.

If that describes the student, the educational licence is straightforwardly good value and you can stop reading. Current pricing for the [Dragon Professional 16 student edition](/dragon-professional-16-student) is on the site.

## Perpetual Licence or Subscription: The Actual Maths

If the student's need is narrower than that — they can use a computer fine, they just cannot get their thoughts onto the page at a reasonable speed — the calculation changes, and it is worth being blunt about it.

Read down that column and the decision gets easier. The perpetual educational licence is better value than the Dragon subscription within the first year, so if you have settled on Dragon, buy the perpetual licence rather than renting it — that one is not a close call.

Between Dragon and a low-cost dictation tool, though, the gap is A$545 once against A$149 a year, and the question becomes how confident you are that the student will actually take to dictating. That is not a given. Dictation is a taught skill, some students find speaking their work deeply unnatural at first, and a proportion try it and go back to typing. Spending A$545 to discover that is an expensive way to find out.

This is why we routinely tell families to trial before they buy, and why a [lower-cost speech to text for students](https://www.speechrecognition.cloud/students) option — which needs no voice-profile training and has a free tier — is often the sensible first purchase for a student who simply needs to dictate. If it works and the student outgrows it, Dragon is still there. If it does not work, you have lost nothing. You can also [compare Dragon versions](/comparison) side by side if you are weighing up which edition fits.

There is one age-related caveat worth knowing. Dragon's recognition is built and tuned primarily around adult speech, and children's and younger teenagers' voices are harder for any speech recognition system — higher pitch, developing articulation, more variable pace. Accuracy generally improves as students get older. A Year 11 or university student will usually have a smoother run with Dragon than a Year 4 student will, which is worth factoring in before committing to a perpetual licence for a younger child.

## Funding It, and When Not to Buy At All

Very few families pay for this out of pocket without checking the alternatives first, and they should not have to.

**NDIS.** Where speech recognition is part of an approved plan as assistive technology, it may be funded. Voice Recognition Australia is an NDIS registered supplier and can advise on how [NDIS funding for assistive technology](https://www.voicerecognition.com.au/pages/ndis-approved-supplier) applies to a specific plan, including what documentation tends to be expected.

**Schools.** Where a school is equipping more than one student, Dragon academic and lab-pack licensing is quoted per deployment rather than bought at shelf price, and it is worth asking. Schools wanting to offer dictation more broadly — a whole cohort rather than the two or three students with the thickest files — usually find volume licensing of a lower-cost tool goes further per dollar than a small number of Dragon seats.

**Assessment and support first.** An occupational therapist is the right referral where the barrier is motor or fatigue-related, and a specialist teacher or psychologist where it is literacy-based. Software bought without that input has a habit of sitting unused.

And the honest counter-case: **do not buy Dragon if the student has not tried dictating yet.** Trial something first, on real homework, for a fortnight. The failure mode we see most often is not the wrong brand — it is a licence bought in good faith for a student who was never shown how to dictate, never taught to proofread what came back, and quietly stopped using it by week three.
